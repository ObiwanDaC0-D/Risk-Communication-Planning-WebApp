## 2. Product Goal

Create a standalone, guided learning-and-planning environment for risk communication planners, cross-functional contributors, reviewers, and planning leads.

The product must:
- Help users develop, revise, review, and assemble a complete risk communication plan.
- Treat communication and engagement as continuous, two-way elements of risk management, not as a final release of information.
- Make each task’s purpose, expected work product, dependencies, and downstream effects visible.
- Preserve traceability among evidence, assumptions, participants, dates, analyses, ratings, decisions, actions, metrics, reviews, and revisions.
- Support human planning-lead and required-office review.
- Never imply that an automated score, checklist, workflow status, or generated output independently approves, validates, releases, or authorizes a decision.

## 6. Seven-Phase Workflow and Fourteen Tasks

Implement this exact workflow:

### Phase 1 — Frame
Task 1. Establish the Planning Charter and Governance
- Work product: planning charter and governance/RACI matrix.
- Capture: decision to support; purpose and need; planning area and scope; planning horizon; authorities, policy, and agreements; decision criteria; planning lead/owner; decision authority; governance functions and RACI assignments.
- Include guidance that identifies required technical, legal/policy, public-engagement, budget, and release coordination as organization-defined responsibilities.

### Phase 2 — Diagnose
Task 2. Baseline Current Conditions and the Future Without Action
- Work product: baseline narrative, outreach inventory, gap map, and future-without-action statement.
- Capture existing engagements, products, events, channels, training, exercises, coordination mechanisms, audiences, owners, frequency, cost/effort, observed reach/results, access barriers, and data gaps.
- Require facts, assumptions, and uncertainty to be distinguishable.

Task 3. Convert Findings into Planning Logic
- Work product: planning-logic table.
- Capture problem, opportunity, objective, constraint, and implication for measures.
- Provide an objective builder using: effect, subject, location, and timing/duration.
- Classify constraints as authority/jurisdiction, information handling, resource, technical uncertainty, or dependency.
- Link planning logic to Tasks 4, 6, 7, and 13. If an entry changes, prompt the user to review affected downstream work.

### Phase 3 — Understand
Task 4. Identify and Segment Stakeholders
- Work product: stakeholder register, priority audience list, and contact-management plan.
- Capture stakeholder/audience; concern; action needed; current relationship; communication need; preferred channel; access accommodation; owner; priority; and contact-management action.
- Explicitly support priority segments with distinct access barriers, authority, readiness, risk, or communication preferences.

Task 5. Calibrate the Required Level of Effort
- Work product: documented level-of-effort determination.
- Support values: maintain, improve, substantially expand, and surge.
- Capture Low/Medium/High factor ratings for risk, preparedness, awareness, capacity, environment, and access complexity.
- Capture evidence, assumptions, participants, date, weighting method, rationale, and sensitivity-check result.
- If scoring is used, show scoring definitions and thresholds. Scores may inform judgment but must never make or approve the decision.

### Phase 4 — Design
Task 6. Build a Library of Management Measures
- Work product: measure library.
- Each measure card must include:
  - Verb-noun measure title.
  - Baseline/mandatory or discretionary designation.
  - Problem/objective addressed.
  - Target audience or area.
  - Key message/content.
  - Delivery method and frequency.
  - Accountable owner and supporting organizations.
  - Prerequisites and dependencies.
  - Cost assumptions: labor, travel, materials, contract support.
  - Measure of performance.
  - Measure of effectiveness.
  - Risks and limitations.
- Prevent baseline activities from being confused with discretionary gap-closing measures.

Task 7. Formulate Meaningfully Different Alternatives
- Work product: alternative packages.
- Require all alternatives to include common baseline measures.
- Require at least three meaningfully different alternative packages, unless a user records a documented exception.
- Capture discretionary measures, target coverage/benefit, resource and schedule assumptions, dependencies, expected contribution, and residual risk.

### Phase 5 — Decide
Task 8. Screen Alternatives for Viability
- Work product: screening record.
- Screen each alternative for policy/constraint compliance, minimum-objective fit, resource ceiling, partner/dependency availability, schedule feasibility, and proportionality.
- Record criterion, result, evidence/rationale, and reason for elimination.
- Do not silently delete screened-out alternatives.

Task 9. Evaluate Alternatives and Document the Selection
- Work product: selection/decision record.
- Support configurable evaluation criteria including acceptability, effectiveness, completeness, and efficiency.
- Require each rating to cite evidence and/or assumptions.
- Show both transparent scoring and a narrative tradeoff analysis covering benefits, limitations, cost, schedule, dependencies, coverage, and residual risk.
- Capture selected alternative, recommendation, decision/approval authority, and selection rationale.
- Make clear that composite scores do not make the decision by themselves.

### Phase 6 — Operationalize
Task 10. Define Scope and Resource the Selected Plan
- Work product: scope of work and budget detail.
- Capture included measures, target groups, owners, frequency, labor hours, unit cost, travel/materials, contract support, total cost, funding office, and period/fiscal-year view.
- Maintain one controlled budget baseline with source, date, assumptions, exclusions, and confidence limits.
- Provide transparent calculations and a visible budget rollup.

Task 11. Build the Implementation Action Matrix
- Work product: executable action matrix.
- Capture measure, action, accountable owner, supporting units, due date/recurrence, status, dependencies, and notes.
- Support action types such as planning, coordination, approval, preparation, delivery, documentation, and assessment.
- Show overdue, blocked, and dependency-risk indicators without falsely implying operational approval.

Task 12. Develop Audience-Specific Key Messages
- Work product: key-message library.
- Capture audience, need to know, needed action, channel/product, limitations/coordination needs, content owner, validation office, release/coordination path, and feedback mechanism.
- Use plain-language and accessibility prompts.
- Ensure messages are audience-specific rather than a generic narrative.

Task 13. Define Performance Measures and Data Collection
- Work product: performance-management plan.
- For each selected measure, require at least one measure of performance and one measure of effectiveness.
- Capture objective/measure, metric, baseline, target, data source/collection method, owner, cadence, priority/underserved-group reach, and reporting method.
- Do not equate activity volume, views, attendance, or product distribution with understanding, readiness, or risk reduction.

### Phase 7 — Learn and Refresh
Task 14. Review, Learn, Revise, and Assemble the Plan
- Work product: annual performance report, AAR tracker, revised action matrix, and assembled final plan.
- Support recurring implementation reviews, annual review, after-action reviews, revision triggers, findings, corrective actions, owners, closure status, and closure dates.
- When new evidence or conditions change the planning basis, identify which earlier tasks may require review.
- Assemble the plan with an executive summary, requested/recorded decision, all work products, and appendices for evidence, stakeholder register, scoring/evaluation, alternatives, action matrix, message library, templates, and AAR records.

## 7. Standard Guided Task Experience

Use the same instructional pattern for every task:

1. Orient
   - Show phase, task title, why the task matters, defined work product, and downstream tasks or decisions affected.

2. Learn
   - Explain the planning concept in concise plain language.
   - Include common errors, misconceptions, and important distinctions.

3. See an Example
   - Provide a short, contextual, hypothetical worked example.
   - Clearly label it “Illustrative example — do not copy as a prescribed answer.”
   - Make it collapsible and hidden by default.

4. Build
   - Use progressive disclosure.
   - Move from context and evidence to analysis, action, and decision-ready output.
   - Use structured fields, repeatable tables, relationships, and links instead of relying solely on free-text narratives.

5. Reflect
   - Present task-specific reflection cards.
   - Include questions related to evidence, uncertainty, feasibility, access, coordination, dependencies, risks, and affected audiences.

6. Review
   - Display completion criteria, unresolved dependencies, readiness state, reviewer feedback, and revision rationale.
   - Allow revision before human review routing.

For Task 3 specifically, include:
- A concise explanation distinguishing a problem, opportunity, objective, constraint, and implication for measures.
- Guided prompts for condition, cause, affected group/system, opportunity, measurable change, and constraint.
- Improvement prompts when an objective lacks effect, subject, location, or timing/duration.
- Reflection questions asking whether each entry is a fact, assumption, or data gap; whether a reviewer can see how the objective follows from the baseline; and whether constraints change feasible measures.
- These prompts must be advisory and educational—not automated judgments or approval gates.

## 8. Readiness, Review, Revision, and Dependencies

Implement clear and accurate states:

- Not started.
- In progress.
- Draft complete.
- Ready for review.
- Returned for revision.
- Complete after recorded human review/validation, where applicable.

Requirements:
- Never use application state to claim independent approval, release authority, legal sufficiency, policy validation, or command decision.
- Readiness checks must indicate missing information, unresolved dependencies, evidence gaps, or self-identified quality concerns.
- Record reviewer feedback, corrective actions, revision rationale, changed fields/records, actor, and timestamp.
- Keep a version history and audit trail of substantive changes.
- When foundational entries change, create a non-blocking dependency review prompt for affected downstream tasks.

Minimum dependency map:
- Task 1 changes may affect all later tasks.
- Tasks 2–3 affect Tasks 4–13.
- Task 4 affects Tasks 5, 6, 7, 12, and 13.
- Task 5 affects Tasks 6–10.
- Tasks 6–7 affect Tasks 8–13.
- Tasks 8–9 affect Tasks 10–14.
- Tasks 10–13 affect Task 14.
- Task 14 may reopen Tasks 1–13.

## 9. UX, Accessibility, and Content Standards

Design for professional staff use:
- Clean, calm, desktop-first workspace that remains usable on tablets and smaller screens.
- Plain language; avoid unnecessary jargon.
- Accessible semantic HTML, keyboard navigation, visible focus states, sufficient contrast, descriptive labels, error summaries, and screen-reader-friendly tables/forms.
- Responsive layouts with no lost functionality on smaller screens.
- Autosave with clear saved/saving/error states.
- Avoid overwhelming users with one long form.
- Use evidence links, dependency badges, plain-language tooltips, status chips, and compact summaries.
- Use fictional, clearly labeled example data only. Do not embed real sensitive, personal, or operational data.

## 12. Acceptance Criteria

The application is acceptable only if it demonstrates all of the following:

1. A user can create a plan and work through all seven phases and fourteen tasks.
2. Every task has the six-part guided experience: Orient, Learn, Example, Build, Reflect, Review.
3. Every task has structured fields and task-specific tables derived from the Workbook.
4. Users can create, edit, link, and trace evidence and assumptions.
5. Users can create stakeholders, measures, alternatives, actions, messages, performance measures, reviews, and corrective actions as first-class records.
6. Users can distinguish baseline/mandatory measures from discretionary measures.
7. Users can screen alternatives, compare viable alternatives, document narrative tradeoffs, and record a human decision authority.
8. Scores, validations, and readiness checks are transparent and advisory; none automatically approves a decision.
9. A material upstream change creates downstream-review prompts.
10. Reviewers can record feedback, corrective actions, revision rationale, and version history.
11. Task 14 can reopen earlier tasks based on a review finding or revision trigger.
12. The plan assembly view includes the complete final-plan checklist and appendices.
13. The UI is accessible, responsive, and free of obvious placeholder-only functionality.
14. The code builds successfully, tests pass, and the local deployment instructions work.
